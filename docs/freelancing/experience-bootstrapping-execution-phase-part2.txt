SECTION 1: DISCOVERY CALL SIMULATION
================================================================================

1.1: CALL OBJECTIVES AND STRUCTURE
----------------------------------

The discovery call has **one main goal**:
• Decide on a **clear, realistic v1** that solves a meaningful slice of the
  client’s problem in a short, low-risk timeframe (here, 3 weeks).

SECONDARY GOALS:
• Build trust and show you understand both the technical and business sides.
• Gather enough information to write a specific proposal (no guessing).
• Qualify that the client is serious, responsive, and aligned with your process.

RECOMMENDED STRUCTURE (20–30 MINUTES)
• 0–3 min: Warm-up, introductions, expectations.
• 3–10 min: Understand current support workflow and pain.
• 10–17 min: Goals, constraints, definition of success.
• 17–23 min: Data, systems, and realistic v1 scope.
• 23–28 min: Next steps, timeline, and how you work.

You don’t explain everything about RAG and agents; you explain **just enough**
to give confidence and keep the focus on outcomes.

1.2: DETAILED CALL SCRIPT (WITH EXAMPLE DIALOG)
-----------------------------------------------

Below is a realistic call script between **you (Ted)** and **Sarah (Head of
Customer Support at AcmeDesk)**. You will adapt this for your own style, but the
structure and questions are reusable.

PART A – OPENING (0–3 MINUTES)
------------------------------
Ted:
• "Hi Sarah, nice to meet you. Thanks for taking the time today."

Sarah:
• "Hi Ted, great to meet you too. Thanks for hopping on."

Ted:
• "Before we dive in, does 30 minutes still work?
   My goal is to understand your current support flow, what success would look
   like for this chatbot, and then I’ll come back with a concise plan and
   milestones. Does that match what you’re hoping to get out of this call?"

Sarah:
• "Yes, that sounds perfect."

PART B – CURRENT SITUATION & PAIN (3–10 MINUTES)
-----------------------------------------------
Ted:
• "Could you walk me through how customer support works today, starting from
   where customers usually start—website, in-app, email—and what tools you use?"

Sarah:
• "Sure. Most tickets come through our Intercom widget and some by email.
   We handle about 200 tickets a day. A lot of them are basic 'How do I do X?'
   questions that are already in our docs."

Ted (drilling down with specific but simple questions):
• "Roughly what percentage would you say are those repetitive questions versus
   more complex issues?"

Sarah:
• "Probably 60%, maybe more."

Ted:
• "Got it. And where does your documentation live today?"

Sarah:
• "We have a public help center with about 120 articles and some internal docs
   in Google Drive that our agents use."

Ted (summarizing to show understanding):
• "So you have a decent knowledge base already, but customers still open tickets
   instead of self-serving, and your team spends a lot of time repeating answers.
   That matches a lot of what I see with other SaaS support teams."

PART C – GOALS AND CONSTRAINTS (10–17 MINUTES)
----------------------------------------------
Ted:
• "If we fast-forward 3–6 months and this project is clearly successful, what
   would be different compared to today? We can talk numbers or just how life
   feels for your team."

Sarah:
• "I’d love to see at least a 50% reduction in those simple tickets so agents
   can focus on the complicated stuff. And we’d like customers to get instant
   answers 24/7."

Ted:
• "That makes sense. Are there any hard constraints we should respect—things
   like brand voice, legal/compliance, where data can be hosted?"

Sarah:
• "We’re careful about pricing and policy answers—AI should never invent them.
   Also, our leadership prefers EU-hosted services if possible, but for a v1
   we can be somewhat flexible if the trade-offs are clear."

Ted:
• "Good to know. On the hallucination side, the approach I take is called RAG—
   Retrieval-Augmented Generation—which means the model only answers by looking
   up your docs first. We can also configure it to say 'I’m not sure' and route
   to a human when the confidence is low, rather than making something up."

PART D – DATA & SYSTEMS (17–23 MINUTES)
---------------------------------------
Ted:
• "For a first version in about 3 weeks, we probably don’t want to touch every
   system you have. If we think in terms of 'minimum viable integration', which
   of these would you like to include in v1, and which can wait?
   - Website chat widget
   - Your public help center
   - Internal Google Docs
   - Your existing ticketing system"

Sarah:
• "Website widget and public help center are must-haves. Internal docs would be
   great if possible. Ticketing system integration can wait."

Ted:
• "Perfect. That lines up well with how I typically structure a v1:
   - A standalone RAG backend that indexes your help center and key internal
     docs.
   - A chat widget on your site that talks only to that backend.
   - No deep CRM/ticketing integration yet; just a safe, self-contained system."

Ted (checking feasibility of 3-week v1):
• "Given your priorities, a 3-week v1 focused on:
   - Chatbot + RAG over help center + selected internal docs
   - Basic admin panel for uploads and analytics
   is realistic. We’d avoid anything that risks derailing that timeline—like
   complex custom routing rules or deep CRM integration—for phase 1.

   Does that level of v1 feel like it would already be valuable for you?"

Sarah:
• "Yes, that’s exactly what we need to start with."

PART E – NEXT STEPS & HOW YOU WORK (23–28 MINUTES)
--------------------------------------------------
Ted:
• "Great. Let me summarize what I heard, and then I’ll outline next steps."

Ted (succinct recap on the call):
• "You’re handling ~200 tickets/day with ~60% repetitive. You have a good help
   center but customers bypass it. Success for v1 is a chatbot on your site that
   can safely answer the top FAQs using your docs, reduce repetitive tickets by
   around half, and run 24/7.

   For v1 in 3 weeks, we’ll keep scope to:
   • Website widget
   • RAG over help center + selected internal docs
   • Simple admin panel with basic analytics

   I’ll put this into a short written plan with architecture, milestones, and

Sarah:
• "Yes, send that over and then we can move forward."

Ted:

   docs and one person who can approve what goes live. Thanks again for the

1.3: HANDLING COMMON QUESTIONS AND OBJECTIONS
--------------------------------------------

During or after the call, clients often ask similar questions. You want **short,
confident answers** that are honest about trade-offs.

OBJECTION 1: "How do we make sure the bot doesn’t hallucinate?"
---------------------------------------------------------------
Client:
• "We’re worried about the chatbot just inventing answers, especially around
   pricing and policies. How do you prevent that?"

You:
• "That’s a valid concern. There are three layers of protection we’ll put in:
  1) Architecture: We use RAG, which means the model always searches your docs
     first and answers based on that retrieved content instead of guessing.
  2) Prompting: We explicitly instruct the model to:
     - Only answer from the provided context.
     - Say 'I’m not sure, let me connect you to a human' when the context
       doesn’t contain an answer.
  3) Guardrails: For sensitive topics like pricing or legal terms, we can:
     - Either disable those categories in v1, or
     - Force escalation to a human every time.

  We’ll also review a sample of conversations in staging before launch so you
  can see exactly how it behaves."

OBJECTION 2: "Can we do this for $X (lower budget)?"
----------------------------------------------------
Client:
• "We were thinking more like $3,000 for this first version. Is that possible?"

You (principled but flexible):
• "I understand wanting to keep initial spend low. The scope we discussed—RAG
   over your help center + internal docs, plus admin panel and analytics—in a
   3-week window lands around $8,000 based on similar projects I’ve done.

   If $3,000 is a hard cap for this phase, we have two options:
   1) Reduce scope: for example, we:
      - Only index the public help center (not internal docs).
      - Skip the admin panel and keep maintenance manual for now.
      - Focus on a simpler analytics view.
   2) Keep scope, extend timeline or split into phases:
      - Phase 1 (~$3,000): basic chatbot + RAG over the help center only.
      - Phase 2: internal docs + admin panel + analytics.

   My recommendation is to keep quality high but adjust scope rather than trying
   to do everything at a lower price and compromising reliability."

OBJECTION 3: "We’re not very technical—how will we manage this after you leave?"
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Client:
• "None of us are very technical. Once this is live, how do we maintain it?"

You:
• "That’s exactly why I include an admin panel and a handover session:
  • Admin panel:
    - Upload new docs.
    - See top questions and simple metrics.
    - Toggle certain content on/off.
  • Handover:
    - 60–90 minute walkthrough recorded on video.
    - Covers: how the system is structured, what you can safely change yourselves
      and what requires a developer.
  • Documentation:
    - Simple runbook in non-technical language for your support lead.

  After that you have two options:
  • Keep me on a light maintenance retainer (a few hours/month).
  • Or just reach out ad-hoc if you need bigger changes later."

2.2: PLAIN-LANGUAGE ARCHITECTURE FOR THE ACMEDESK CHATBOT
---------------------------------------------------------

You will describe the architecture in terms a non-technical Head of Support
understands, using **business analogies** where helpful.

EXAMPLE DESCRIPTION
-------------------
"At a high level, the solution has three main pieces:

1) Website chat widget (what your customers see)
   • A modern chat bubble on your website that customers can click to ask
     questions in natural language.
   • It matches your branding and works on desktop and mobile.

2) AI brain connected to your documentation (RAG backend)
   • Behind the scenes, there is a service that:
     - Regularly reads and indexes your help center and selected internal docs.
     - Stores them in a special search index designed for AI (a vector database).
     - When a customer asks a question, it:
       1) Finds the most relevant passages from your docs.
       2) Feeds those passages into the AI model.
       3) Asks the model to answer **only** based on that information.

   • This is called Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): instead of guessing,
     the AI looks things up in your content first, then answers.

3) Admin panel for your team (control and visibility)
   • A simple web dashboard where your support lead can:
     - Upload new documents or mark old ones as outdated.
     - See which questions customers ask most often.
     - Monitor basic usage and performance.

This setup keeps the first version:
• Safe: answers grounded in your docs.
• Maintainable: your team can update content without code changes.
• Extensible: later we can plug the same 'brain' into Intercom, email, or
  internal tools without rebuilding from scratch."

You can later attach a simple diagram, but the **words** above should make sense
on their own.

2.3: TECHNICAL APPENDIX (OPTIONAL)
----------------------------------

For more technical stakeholders (CTO, senior engineers), you can add a short
appendix either in the same email or as a separate document.

EXAMPLE TECHNICAL APPENDIX OUTLINE
----------------------------------
• Stack:
  - Front-end: Next.js + Tailwind, embedded as a JS widget.
  - Backend: FastAPI (Python) or Node/Express.
  - RAG: LangChain + Chroma/Pinecone.
  - DB: Postgres for conversations + metadata.
  - Hosting: Vercel + Railway (or EU-hosted alternatives).

• Data flow:
  1) Nightly/periodic ingestion of help center + selected Google Docs.
  2) Chunking + embeddings stored in vector DB.
  3) Runtime:
     - User question → backend.
     - Top-k semantic matches retrieved.
     - Prompt constructed with retrieved context.
     - OpenAI GPT-4 generates answer.
     - Answer + context stored for analytics.

• Guardrails:
  - System prompt enforcing grounded answers.
  - Confidence threshold with escalation path.
  - Optional category-level guards (e.g., pricing).

This appendix is not required for every client, but having a template ready
means you can quickly add it when you notice a technical stakeholder.

3.4: HANDLING SCOPE CHANGES AND CHANGE REQUESTS
-----------------------------------------------

Inevitably, clients will ask:
• "Can we also have X?" mid-project.

You need a polite but firm way to:
• Protect your time.
• Keep the current milestones realistic.
• Still be flexible and helpful.

EXAMPLE RESPONSE (SCOPE CHANGE MID-WEEK)
----------------------------------------
Client:
• "Can we also have the bot integrated with Zendesk in this first version?"

You:
• "That’s a good idea and definitely possible technically.

   For the 3-week v1 we agreed to focus on the website widget + RAG backend +
   admin panel so that we can ship something reliable quickly.

   Integrating with Zendesk adds:
   • Additional development and testing work (APIs, auth, edge cases).
   • Extra complexity for the first launch.

   I’d suggest we keep the current v1 scope as-is and treat Zendesk integration
   as the first item for Phase 2. If you’d like, I can include a rough estimate
   for that in the handover so you know what to expect."

For larger scopes or bigger changes, you can propose:
• "We can add a small change-budget on an hourly basis."
or
• "We can create a new milestone for this and adjust the timeline."
